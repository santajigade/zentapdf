A bill of materials (BOM) lists every part, sub-assembly and material needed to make one finished product, with how many of each. This Excel template is a multi-level BOM on one sheet. You enter the parts, quantities and unit costs, and it works out the cost per finished unit, the quantity to buy for a production run with scrap added, and the cost of that run.

How to use this bill of materials template

  1. Download the Excel file and open it in Microsoft Excel, Google Sheets or LibreOffice Calc.
  2. Fill in the product details at the top: name, code, revision, date and who prepared and approved it.
  3. Type the number of finished units you plan to make in Build qty.
  4. Delete the desk lamp sample lines, then add your parts. Use Level 1 for the main assemblies and Level 2 or 3 for the parts that go into them.
  5. For each line, pick Make, Buy or Subcontract, then enter the quantity needed for one finished product and the unit.
  6. Enter the unit cost on Buy and Subcontract lines. Leave it blank on Make lines, because their cost comes from the parts under them.
  7. Add a scrap % where some material is lost or rejected, plus supplier and lead time if you track them.
  8. Check the cost per finished unit at the top and the cost by source table at the bottom, then print or save as PDF.

What is in the sheet

The BOM sheet has a header for product details and four boxes at the top: number of line items, cost per finished unit, parts cost for the build quantity, and how many lines you buy or subcontract.

The table has 30 lines with these columns: Level, Part number, Part name and description, Make or buy, Qty per unit, Unit, Unit cost, Cost per unit, Scrap %, Qty for build, Build cost, Supplier, Lead time (days) and Notes. Level 1 rows are shaded so the main assemblies stand out. Under the totals, a small table splits the cost per unit into Buy, Subcontract and Make.

How the formulas work

Cost per unit is Qty per unit multiplied by Unit cost. Qty for build is Qty per unit multiplied by Build qty, plus the scrap %, rounded up to a whole number. Build cost is Qty for build multiplied by Unit cost.

Because every quantity is per one finished product, a part used in two assemblies can be listed twice and the totals still add up. Do not put a cost on a Make line as well as on its parts, or that cost will be counted twice.

Single level or multi-level

A single level BOM is a flat list of parts, which is fine for a simple product. A multi-level BOM groups parts under the assemblies they belong to, so it is easier to see where a part is used when a design changes. This sheet works both ways: put every line at Level 1 for a flat list.

Labour and overheads are not included, so add them when you price the product.

Tips for best results

  • Give every part a unique part number and never reuse an old one for a different part.
  • Change the revision letter and date each time the BOM changes, and keep the old file.
  • Include small items such as screws, labels and packing material, which are easy to forget when ordering.

Frequently asked questions

What is a bill of materials?

A list of all the parts, materials and sub-assemblies needed to make one unit of a product, with quantities, and often costs and suppliers.

What does Qty per unit mean in this sheet?

The quantity needed for one finished product, not for one sub-assembly. If an arm assembly uses two tubes and the lamp has one arm, enter 2.

How is scrap added?

Qty for build adds the scrap % on top. With 6 screws per unit, 500 units and 5% scrap, it shows 3,150.

Can I add more than 30 lines?

Yes. Insert rows inside the table, above the Totals row, and copy the formulas from the row above so the totals include them.
